Turning Brastemp's generic product page into a premium buying journey

A redesign shaped by award-winning benchmarks and behavioral data, rebuilt inside a new design system on a launch deadline that would not move.

Context

Brastemp's new premium refrigerators sat on a generic product page. Value went unperceived, sales leaned on discounts, and leadership had named the redesign one of the company's top 10 initiatives.

Challenge

Lift conversion on a high-ticket product and give the page a bespoke, aspirational, premium feel worthy of the new positioning. All on a fixed launch timeline, mid-migration to a new design system, and with hundreds of possible directions to choose from.

My direct contribution

I led it end to end: the discovery (competitor benchmark, behavioral data, CES survey), the redesign from generic catalog to a real buying journey, and a high-fidelity prototype built on the new design system's tokens and components. I coordinated my product squad and partnered with Design Ops, Content, and Data. My hardest call was defending, with the Insurance & Services team, that the page should secure the product's conversion before selling the add-on.

01 · Challenge and context

One of the company's top 10 initiatives

Mad Max was Brastemp's move to reposition the brand into aspirational territory, alongside the launch of its premium appliance line. Leadership defined it as one of the company's top 10 initiatives, in their own words:

We want to recreate our Product and Category pages with more immersive and interactive content and communication, simplifying the way we present our products.

02 · Discovery · 2 weeks

Evidence before design

My first move here was to reduce risk: to find the real opportunity inside the space that had just opened up.

I grounded discovery in three axes:

  1. A competitor benchmark of award-winning bespoke product pages
  2. Module-level interaction-versus-conversion data for the current page
  3. A Customer Effort Score (CES) survey

From the benchmark, six patterns stood out:

  • 01Apple, BORA, Oura

    Scroll storytelling

    The page as a cinematic narrative, where each scroll reveals a chapter.

  • 02Oura, Miele, Bellroy

    Progressive disclosure

    Overview on the surface, detail on demand.

  • 03Apple, Miele, BORA

    Product as hero

    Cinematic visual treatment, aspirational styled photography.

  • 04Bellroy, Miele

    Interactive explorer

    Modules you navigate by angle or feature, where the user investigates rather than scrolls past.

  • 05Nothing, Apple

    Design equals identity

    The product’s visual language dominates the whole digital experience.

  • 06Bellroy, BORA

    Interactive proof over claims

    Value shown with visual tools and short video, not just text.

During this stage I also mapped the dependency that worried me most: our graphic materials were simple next to the competitors'. Without premium photography and per-feature video, scroll storytelling had nothing to fill the screen. That became a work stream of its own.

A UX critique of the current product page then confirmed, from the UX side, exactly what the kickoff had flagged from the business side: static content, value not perceived, over-reliance on incentives.

What's missing to reach the next level: Bespoke Aspirational

Critical gap6
  • No scroll storytelling
  • No progressive disclosure
  • Cross-sell display strategy (Insurance & Services + parts)
  • Per-feature video, mobile-first and desktop, in use
  • Photos without a fullscreen / zoom modal
  • Pop-up banner at the wrong moment, interrupting the journey
To improve4
  • Premium visual design of the page
  • Product description, highlights and microcopy
  • Differentiators and features carousel
  • Technical information / spec sheet

Two of those critical gaps were not mine to fix on a screen. The line launched with just 2 SKUs, small enough to produce the missing photography and video by hand, but more launches were coming, so I did not solve for two. I gave the content team a production guideline built to serve every future launch, and defended the budget for a new image set by arguing its reuse across inbound and outbound.

03 · Data analysis and problem space

Which modules actually move someone toward the purchase?

The benchmark opened more directions than one page could hold. Crossing them against how the current page actually behaved is what turned a list of possibilities into where to act, what to change, and why.

Three sources, none of them answering the same question:

  • GA4. What people did on the page, module by module.
  • Hotjar. How they did it: where they stalled, what they scrolled past, what they never reached at all.
  • CES survey. What they said about it afterwards.

I ran the behavioural cuts with the Data team, and built the personas with our UX Researcher from the two together.

Each one settled a different question:

  1. The personas settled engagement opportunities. The same funnel carries someone who will not click and someone who will not commit without proof, and a page that picks one of them loses the other.
  2. The CES settled friction and pain points. Effort, in two forms: hunting for the truth, then getting past the noise. Both are the page's fault, and both are fixable.
  3. The lift settled where to focus our effort. Not on the highest scores, which only record decisions already made, but on the differentiators, the one module where content creates value instead of capturing it.

That left me with hypotheses rather than convictions, which is the point: make the basics findable and the Validator stops leaving to search for them; expose the differentiators instead of hiding them behind a click and the Window Shopper goes deeper; move the add-on behind the decision and the product's own conversion rises without the attach collapsing. Wave 1 tested the cheapest of the three before anyone spent a sprint on the others.

04 · Wave 1 · quick win on the current product page

Validate the direction cheaply, before investing heavily

The launch dates would not wait for the Orbit roadmap, so I validated the direction on the old site first. The central move was a new differentiators module: collapsed, click-to-open sections swapped for open ones in an alternating image-and-text sequence, in vibrant marsala. The bet was simple: expose the content the data called high value, instead of hiding it.

05 · Wave 2 · full redesign in Orbit

Where to invest, where to reuse

Before the A/B test even closed, the job shifted: v2 was the whole page rebuilt inside Orbit, a reusable blueprint rather than a one-off screen. Orbit lived in Storybook as a library of components, so I composed each module inside its logic, reusing agnostic components where their quality held and building from tokens, atoms, and molecules where it did not, negotiating with Design Ops which parts they would ship and which were mine. My rule for spending: new-build budget only where impact was high and nothing existing solved it. Everything else, recompose or reuse.

The new first fold redesigned

  • For the Window Shopper, hook, not close. Expanded premium photos and a looping video build desire. Scroll depth rose 51%, and readers who pass 50% of the page convert about 45% above average.
  • For the Validator, resolve objections, fast. A dimensions photo and anchor chips jump straight to the specs and the modules that follow.

Reused, not rebuilt

The old reviews block was legacy VTEX. Instead of designing a new one, I reused the component Orbit had already shipped for Whirlpool Colombia's launch: aggregate rating, score distribution, filters, and verified purchase, all inherited. The lift was positive but moderate (+65%), so it didn't earn a new build. It earned good reuse.

The remaining modules followed the same logic. FAQ and nearby alternatives were anchored in the real objections: voltage, installation, warranty. The cross-sell relocation was specified and never shipped, which is the subject of the last section.

The showpiece

Product Tour

A styled kitchen with the refrigerator at the center, navigation by feature, and a layered feel that puts the user inside the scene. My most deliberate call was an automatic timer, 5 seconds per feature, so each technology presents itself without a click.

  • It answers the number-one CES pain. Hunting for technical information. The timer presents each feature without asking for a click.
  • One module, two jobs. It carries the Window Shopper through features they would never have clicked, which buys depth. It is a showroom for the Validator, who can ignore the timer and take each feature apart at their own pace.

Honesty about causation

The gains do not come from the layout alone. Parallel initiatives, such as Project Themis (tracking accuracy) and the Complementary Feed (qualified traffic via regional offers), were fundamental levers. The cleanest causal reading comes from a category where price was neutral and the decollapsed layout still beat the average. That is the argument I stand behind without caveats.

What we still need to measure. Wave 1 proved that exposing the content was worth doing, which is what justified building the rest. What it cannot say is what the rebuilt page is worth. The full v2 in Orbit has not closed, and that figure will follow here when it does.

08 · Reflection and next steps

The gap I did not close

Moving Insurance & Services out of the first fold was my hardest call, and I treated it as a decision about a page. It was a decision about an organisation. Warranties, installation, trade-in and parts belong to other teams with their own targets, and a display strategy that settles where they live has to be built with those teams, not defended against them. I made the case from both sides, the behavioural data and the CES, and designed the move as a controlled test on a single SKU so that nobody had to take it on faith. It never ran. Those teams were absorbed by the platform migration and the capacity was not there for this front, so the module is still in the first fold and the proposal is still a proposal. That gap is the thing standing between this page and the branded journey it was meant to become.

It also helps to be exact about how far this went. On the maturity ladder I use for premium pages, the page shipped at stage three of five.

So I took it to the PM and the managers as a production problem rather than a design one, with two proposals. Every launch ships two cuts of each feature video: one with burned-in text for social, and one clean, usable in a product page, a landing page or the catalogue. And one track from launch kickoff to live, where design, content and marketing agree the asset matrix per feature up front, instead of each channel commissioning its own. The production already happens. It is just fragmented, so the model reorganises it rather than adding to it.
