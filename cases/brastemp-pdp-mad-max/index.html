Context

Brastemp's new premium refrigerators sat on a generic product page. Value went unperceived, sales leaned on discounts, and leadership had named the redesign one of the company's top 10 initiatives.

Challenge

Lift conversion on a high-ticket product and give the page a bespoke, aspirational, premium feel worthy of the new positioning. All on a fixed launch timeline, mid-migration to a new design system, and with hundreds of possible directions to choose from.

My direct contribution

I led it end to end: the discovery (competitor benchmark, behavioral data, CES survey), the redesign from generic catalog to a real buying journey, and a high-fidelity prototype built on the new design system's tokens and components. I coordinated my product squad and partnered with Design Ops, Content, and Data. My hardest call was defending, with the Insurance & Services team, that the page should secure the product's conversion before selling the add-on.

01 · Challenge and context

One of the company's top 10 initiatives

Mad Max was Brastemp's move to reposition the brand into aspirational territory, alongside the launch of its premium appliance line. Leadership defined it as one of the company's top 10 initiatives, in their own words:

We want to recreate our Product and Category pages with more immersive and interactive content and communication, simplifying the way we present our products.

The goal had two layers: reduce the reliance on commercial levers to drive sales, and make the new positioning tangible, with a wow effect on the product features and the differentiator blocks. It was also a first for the company: Brastemp had never examined its own content against consumer studies before. That exercise produced the transformation compass that guided the mission:

AS-ISTO-BE
Technical barrier: complex features, value not perceivedAssertive communication: spotlight on differentiators and features
Reliance on incentives: selling on discount, coupon, shippingImmersive content: inspirational, interactive product pages
Engagement gap: static content, technical languageBrand and consumer connection: solving everyday pains

Two constraints framed the project from day one. The timeline would not move: launch dates and the brand's anniversary were fixed. And a design-system migration ran in parallel, so everything had to ship during the switch from Brastemp's legacy DS to Orbit, a brand-agnostic system built to migrate every brand's sites by the end of 2026.

I ran this as Senior Product Designer inside a cross-product squad, in direct partnership with Design Ops, Research, and Content Production.

02 · Discovery · 2 weeks

Evidence before design

My first move here was to reduce risk: to find the real opportunity inside the space that had just opened up. One rule I tried to set for myself was that every choice should be grounded in data, and every solution refined with the technical and content teams.

I grounded discovery in three axes:

  1. A competitor benchmark of award-winning bespoke product pages
  2. Module-level interaction-versus-conversion data for the current page
  3. A Customer Effort Score (CES) survey

From the benchmark, six patterns stood out:

  • 01Apple, BORA, Oura

    Scroll storytelling

    The page as a cinematic narrative, where each scroll reveals a chapter.

  • 02Oura, Miele, Bellroy

    Progressive disclosure

    Overview on the surface, detail on demand.

  • 03Apple, Miele, BORA

    Product as hero

    Cinematic visual treatment, aspirational styled photography.

  • 04Bellroy, Miele

    Interactive explorer

    Modules you navigate by angle or feature, where the user investigates rather than scrolls past.

  • 05Nothing, Apple

    Design equals identity

    The product’s visual language dominates the whole digital experience.

  • 06Bellroy, BORA

    Interactive proof over claims

    Value shown with visual tools and short video, not just text.

During this stage I also mapped the dependency that worried me most: our graphic materials were simple next to the competitors'. Without premium photography and per-feature video, scroll storytelling had nothing to fill the screen. That became a work stream of its own (section 3).

A UX critique of the current product page then confirmed, from the UX side, exactly what the kickoff had flagged from the business side: static content, value not perceived, over-reliance on incentives.

03 · Cross challenges · beyond the product team

Two blockers no screen could fix

The current product page was not bad. It was generic. A page can be flawless in Figma and still fail for reasons that live outside the file. Two of them did.

Challenge 1: no styled imagery or product video

The line launched with just 2 SKUs, small enough to create the missing content by hand. But more launches were coming, so I did not solve for two. I gave the content team a production guideline built to serve every future launch, and defended the budget for a new image set by arguing its reuse across inbound and outbound:

  • Styled premium content: photography in a real kitchen, mobile-first (9:19.5) and desktop (16:9).
  • Self-explanatory looping videos: 4 to 5 short videos per killer feature.

Challenge 2: development agility in VTEX

Historically, custom modules were built to order, hard to reuse, and slow. The new design system, Orbit, was being built to live inside Storybook as a library of reusable components.

So I bet on building every new module inside its logic: reuse agnostic components where quality held, compose from tokens, atoms, and molecules where it did not, negotiating with Design Ops which parts they would build and which were mine.

The result was speed for development and a page that doubled as a reusable blueprint.

04 · Data analysis and problem space

Which modules actually move someone toward the purchase?

This phase was not about how to build. It was about knowing what to build, and why.

These aren't two people. They're two modes of the same shopper: the same person browses in one session and validates in another. So the page couldn't pick a side, it had to move someone from one mode to the next, in order.

CES · Customer Effort Score

The survey's complaints clustered into two themes that accounted for most of the friction. Together they describe one problem: the old page asked too much effort, first to find the truth, then to get past the noise.

Hidden or missing technical information

What people saidCouldn't find the technical specs; features, differentiators, photos, and reviews didn't help them validate.

Information like the height should be at the top. Hunting for it is exhausting, I'll give up.

The design callMake technical information easy to find: the Product Tour and a dimensions module.

Visual clutter: pop-ups and text overload

What people saidBugs and pop-ups that won't close, and dense, hard-to-parse content, worse on mobile.

Too many pop-ups in your face in front of what you want to see, and you just give up.

The design callStrip the visual clutter, and move the dense add-on out of the first fold.

That still left the question no one had answered with data: which modules actually move someone toward the purchase? The key metric was lift.

In plain terms: warranty/installation at +1,344% means buyers touched that module about 14 times more than non-buyers. It is the trail of someone who already decided. The gallery, with negative lift, is the opposite: it is where browsers linger before leaving. The gallery does not push buyers away; buyers simply do not need it. Engagement with photos is browsing behavior, not buying behavior.

The opportunity ahead

For the future premium product page, my biggest opportunity here was to invest where there was room to create value, the product features differentiators, the only content that doubles the engagement of people who really want to buy, but are unsure if it's the right product.

05 · Wave 1 · quick win on the current product page

Validate the direction cheaply, before investing heavily

The launch dates would not wait for the Orbit roadmap, so I validated the direction on the old site first. The central move was a new differentiators module: collapsed, click-to-open sections swapped for open ones in an alternating image-and-text sequence, in vibrant marsala. The bet was simple: expose the content the data called high value, instead of hiding it.

06 · Wave 2 · full redesign in Orbit

Where to invest, where to reuse

Even before the A/B test closed, the data had me confident. For v2 the job shifted: same rationale, but redesign the whole page inside Orbit and leave a reusable blueprint, not a one-off screen.

Every module was a cost decision. I built a prioritization matrix with product and dev, crossing impact on conversion against build effort. The rule: spend new-build budget only where impact is high and nothing existing solves it. Everything else, recompose or reuse.

A symptom, not a sentence

The obvious move on a −31.65% module is to shrink it. But that lift was measured on the old gallery: 21 loose photos on a white background, no narrative. The data didn't say images don't sell. It said this gallery doesn't sell. It did neither job: it didn't hook the Window Shopper, and it didn't resolve the Validator's questions. So I gave it a clear job, one per persona:

  • For the Window Shopper, hook, not close. Expanded premium photos and a looping video build desire. Scroll depth rose 51%, and readers who pass 50% of the page convert about 45% above average.
  • For the Validator, resolve objections, fast. A dimensions photo and anchor chips jump straight to the specs and the modules that follow.

Reused, not rebuilt

The old reviews block was legacy VTEX. Instead of designing a new one, I reused the component Orbit had already shipped for Whirlpool Colombia's launch: aggregate rating, score distribution, filters, and verified purchase, all inherited. The lift was positive but moderate (+65%), so it didn't earn a new build. It earned good reuse.

The remaining modules followed the same logic, all moved out of the decision path: cross-sell relocated to the end, and FAQ and nearby alternatives anchored in the real objections (voltage, installation, warranty).

The showpiece

Product Tour

A styled kitchen with the refrigerator at the center, navigation by feature, and a layered feel that puts the user inside the scene. My most deliberate call was an automatic timer, 5 seconds per feature, so each technology presents itself without a click. It serves both personas at once: it works for the Window Shopper who will not click, and it improves the findability the Validator needs, answering the number-one CES pain directly.

It almost did not ship. Stretched thin by the design-system migration, development was about to cut it. I took it straight to Whirlpool leadership and secured the buy-in that protected the most complex, most strategic module on the page. I built it with Figma Make and Claude Code for the interactive prototype and GSAP tests, then shipped it in Orbit.

07 · Trade-offs

Three conscious trade-offs

  • Securing the product's conversion before the add-on sell — my hardest call. The old first fold pushed warranties and installation up front. That module has huge lift, but the lift is the fingerprint of buyers who already decided, not the cause of the decision. Moving Insurance & Services out of the first fold was close to taboo internally: it's a revenue line with its own KPI. I made the case as one-team thinking, not each of us guarding a metric in isolation. The CES survey backed this from the user side: the dense add-on was part of the visual clutter shoppers complained about, so moving it out of the first fold also cut the noise they were fighting. I de-risked it instead of betting: I did not remove the module, I repositioned it and measured it in a controlled test on a single SKU.
  • Execution time vs. ideal quality. I accepted content gaps (per-feature video moved to a v2) and prioritized modules with a good fit in Orbit. Video has moderate lift (+36%), so its absence is a wow gap, not a conversion gap.
  • Speed vs. preliminary user validation. We gave up a moderated usability test and chose to validate via A/B. Since the data analysis and the benchmark converged on the same direction, risk was already partially mitigated.

Honesty about causation

The gains do not come from the layout alone. Parallel initiatives, such as Project Themis (tracking accuracy) and the Complementary Feed (qualified traffic via regional offers), were fundamental levers. The cleanest causal reading comes from a category where price was neutral and the decollapsed layout still beat the average. That is the argument I stand behind without caveats.

What we still need to measure. The full v2 (Orbit) has not closed yet. The open question: did pulling the parts add-on and Insurance & Services out of the first fold hurt total revenue enough to outweigh the product's conversion gain? That is exactly why the move was designed as a measured experiment (treatment vs. control, stable price). Once v2 confirms, a revenue figure will follow here.

09 · Reflection and next steps

What I would do differently

This case stretched me. I led alignment across content, data, design ops, and Whirlpool leadership, and my Design Manager's explicit recognition gave me the confidence to keep pushing on the real blockers and how to get around them.

The deliverable I would most have wanted more time for is a new user journey map. With the new performance data and the personas in hand, revisiting that map would surface end-to-end opportunities across the consideration and decision stages, and turn what I learned on this product page into direction for the whole journey.
